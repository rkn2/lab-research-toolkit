This guide covers every major formal checkpoint in your PhD — from your first committee meeting through graduation. Read the whole thing early so you know what is coming. Return to each section when you are approaching that milestone.

## Committee Meetings

Your doctoral committee meets at least once a year to review your progress, give feedback on your direction, and sign off on your advancement.

### What to Prepare

You will produce two things for every committee meeting: a slide presentation and a written report.

**Presentation (15 min)**

- 1–2 slides: introduction and research questions (remind the committee of your focus)
- 1–2 slides per published paper
- Current and future research — this is the meat of the talk; spend the most time here
- Timeline for completion — include both your original projected timeline and an updated one showing where you actually are

**Written Report**

Submit the report to the Graduate School after the meeting. Structure it as follows:

1. **Introduction** — one paragraph motivation for your work
2. **Research questions** — your explicit research questions in numbered format
3. **Publications and submissions to date**
   - Journal papers: citation, abstract, and research questions for each
   - Conference papers: citations only
4. **Current research** — for each active topic, a status update with results and next steps
5. **Timeline for completion** — include the original image from your comps, the updated image, projected journal and conference papers, time to write the dissertation, projected graduation date, and any other experiences you want to fit in before then
6. **Appendix** — full journal papers

## Comprehensive Exam

The comprehensive exam is the transition from coursework to full-time research. Passing it makes you a PhD Candidate. It has two parts: a written proposal and an oral defense.

### The Written Proposal

The proposal demonstrates that you understand your field, have identified a meaningful gap in the literature, and have a credible plan to address it.

Use this structure:

**Front matter**
- Title page: title, your name, adviser name, committee list, date
- Abstract
- Table of contents
- List of Figures, Tables, Equations, Symbols

**Introduction**
- *Motivation:* "The global problem my work addresses is X. An obstacle is Y. Thus, a smaller goal is Z. This is hard because W."
- *Research objectives:* "The aim of this work is to X." Then 1–4 numbered research questions.
- *Proposal organization and novel contributions to the field*

**Literature review**
- Organized into subsections by topic
- End with a summary and explicit statement of the research gap

**Research goals / hypothesis**
- "This research aims to X." (1–2 sentences, matching the research objectives above)
- "This is novel because X." (1–2 sentences)

**Proposed research methodology**
- Potential workflow diagram
- Tools necessary to undertake the proposed research

**Current research**
- One section per completed or in-progress component, each tied to a journal paper in review or revision. For example: "This section is based on the following paper in review: [Author], '[Title],' [Journal], [Year]."

**Future work**
- One section per planned component

**Expected novel contributions**
- What the proposed research will advance
- What it will establish
- Why it is transformative, what new tools it offers, what new research questions it enables

**Anticipated schedule**
- Target graduation date
- Include factors of safety — build in buffer
- Anticipated conference presentations and proceedings
- Projected journal publications
- Projected professional development activities

## Dissertation

In this lab, dissertations are typically built from published or submitted journal papers — meaning if you have written the papers, most of the dissertation is already written.

### The Papers-to-Chapters Model

Your dissertation chapter structure depends on how your methods and case studies relate across your papers. Choose the structure that fits your work:

**If methods are consistent across all chapters** — pull the methods out into a shared chapter that precedes the research chapters.

**If methods are distinct across all chapters** — keep methods inside each chapter; include a case study section if relevant.

**If the case study is the same but methods differ** — a shared case study chapter precedes the methods and results chapters.

**If both case study and methods are consistent** — shared case study and methods chapters, followed by results chapters.

In all structures, the dissertation wraps the papers in a unified frame:

| Chapter | Content |
|---|---|
| Introduction | Research objectives (1.1) and dissertation organization and novel contributions (1.2) |
| Literature review | Covering the field across all papers |
| Case study (if shared) | Described once, referenced throughout |
| Methods (if shared) | Described once, referenced throughout |
| Chapter n, n+1, … | One per paper — adapted for the dissertation context |
| Conclusions | Summary of conclusions from each paper + future work |

**On the conclusions chapter:** Start from your introduction and rephrase it as a summary of what was accomplished. Then summarize the conclusions from each paper. If you described future work in individual papers but have not yet completed it, you can consolidate it here.

**On transitions:** Make sure chapter-to-chapter transitions are smooth and not redundant. You can have chapter-specific acknowledgements if different papers had different collaborators.

## Graduating

Finishing a PhD has more logistics than most people expect. Start this checklist at least one semester before your target graduation date.

### Lab Offboarding: Data and File Handoff

Before you leave, your work must be organized so Rebecca can access everything after you are gone. You will have a folder in Rebecca's Penn State account. At the top level it should contain at minimum your meeting notes and a documents folder.

**Meeting notes folder** — PDFs or Word docs you shared with Rebecca each week, organized by date: `year_mm_dd_meetingnotes`. Include any annual evaluations or progress markers.

**Documents folder** with these subfolders:

- **Papers (`_publications`):** Final PDF versions of all your papers. Conference papers go in a subfolder.

- **Project folders (`project_1_name`, etc.):** One folder per research project, each containing:
  - *Code:* Everything needed to rerun your results. Links to public git repositories are fine, but also include archived static copies of the Python or notebook files. All code must be documented. Use the readme if there is anything to explain.
  - *Data:* Everything needed to rerun your results. Links to public repositories (Zenodo, DesignSafe) are fine, but include archived copies unless the data is too large — in which case, document the alternative in the readme.
  - *publication_info:* Submission, review, and revision records. One subfolder per journal you submitted to, with `accepted` or `rejected` flags. Both overleaf links and downloaded files are required for each.

- **Presentations:** Organized as `presentations / year / conferenceName / ppt` (and paper if relevant, with figures and text in editable form).

- **Mentoring:** If you mentored an undergraduate researcher, create a folder `year_First_Last` with any datasets or materials from their work. The readme should explain how their data connects to your papers.

- **Teaching:** If you TA'd or taught any courses, upload materials here organized by course name and year.
