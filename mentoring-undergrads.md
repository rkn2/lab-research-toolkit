# Mentoring an Undergraduate Researcher

At some point in your PhD you will likely mentor an undergraduate researcher. This is one of the most valuable things you will do — not just for them, but for you. Learning to scope work for someone else, communicate expectations, and get useful output from a less experienced researcher is the core skill of running a lab someday. It is also, done well, genuinely fun.

This guide is how to do it well. It connects to the [offboarding checklist](phd-milestones.md#lab-offboarding-data-and-file-handoff), which already assumes you will have mentored undergraduates and asks you to document their work.

## What Mentoring Actually Is

Mentoring is not delegation. You are not handing off tasks you do not want to do — you are teaching someone to do research while getting real work done. That reframe changes everything about how you spend the time.

The most common mistake new mentors make is assuming the undergraduate knows things they do not. They usually do not yet know how to read a paper, how to structure code, why reproducibility matters, or what "analyze this data" concretely means. Part of your job is to teach those things — and teaching them is how you learn whether you actually understand them yourself. Point them at the relevant guides in this toolkit; most of them work as well for a motivated undergraduate as for a new PhD student.

Your other job is to make them feel like a real member of the lab. An undergraduate who feels valued does dramatically better work than one who feels like free labor.

## Scoping an Undergrad-Sized Project

The hardest part of mentoring is defining a project that is real but bounded. Aim for work that:

- **Has a clear, concrete deliverable.** "Help with my research" is not a project. "Build a labeled dataset of façade damage from these 200 images, following this rubric" is.
- **Fits their available hours.** An undergraduate typically has a handful of hours a week, often around coursework. Scope for that reality, not for a full-time researcher.
- **Connects to your work but is not on its critical path.** Their work should matter and feed into your research — but if it slips, your dissertation should not be blocked. Give them something real, not something so load-bearing that a bad week for them becomes a crisis for you.
- **Has a gentle on-ramp.** Start with a small, well-defined first task they can complete and feel good about, before scaling up. Early wins build confidence and tell you a lot about how much scaffolding they need.

Check your proposed scope with Rebecca before you hand it off, the same way you would [check any new direction](weekly-meetings.md#starting-something-new). She has seen more undergrad projects succeed and fail than you have.

## A Weekly Cadence

Borrow the structure that works for your own advising, scaled down. A lightweight weekly rhythm:

- **A short weekly check-in.** 20–30 minutes. What did they do, what is blocking them, what is next. This mirrors the lab's own [weekly meeting](weekly-meetings.md) structure and models good research habits for them.
- **A shared place for their work.** A folder or repo where their code, data, and notes live — set up from the start, not retrofitted at the end. This is also what makes documentation (below) painless.
- **Clear next steps every week.** Undergraduates, even more than PhD students, benefit from leaving each meeting knowing exactly what to do next. Ambiguity that a senior researcher can navigate will stall a beginner.

Be responsive between meetings, but also teach them to batch questions rather than pinging you constantly — a skill they will need for the rest of their career.

## Documenting Their Work

From the first week, have your mentee keep their work organized and reproducible. This is not busywork — it is what lets their contribution actually feed into your papers, and it is required at offboarding.

The [offboarding checklist](phd-milestones.md#lab-offboarding-data-and-file-handoff) specifies that mentored-undergraduate work is handed off in a folder named `year_First_Last`, containing their datasets and materials, with a README explaining how their data connects to your papers. If you keep their work organized as you go — following the same [reproducible-research](reproducible-research.md) habits you use — this folder assembles itself. If you do not, you will be reconstructing months of someone else's undocumented work right when you are trying to graduate.

Teach them the basics of [Git and GitHub](git-and-github.md) and [reproducible research](reproducible-research.md) early. It is a gift to them and an insurance policy for you.

## Authorship and Credit

If an undergraduate makes a substantive intellectual contribution to a paper, they should be an author. Early-career authorship is enormously valuable to an undergraduate — it can be the thing that gets them into a strong graduate program — and the cost to you of including a deserving contributor is essentially zero.

Be clear and honest about this from the start. Do not dangle authorship as a vague reward, and do not quietly drop a contributor who earned it. The [Authorship and Collaboration](authorship.md) guide applies to your mentee exactly as it applies to any collaborator: talk about it early, use the CRediT roles to keep it concrete, and revisit it if their contribution grows.

## Posters, Letters, and What They Get Out of It

Your mentee is investing in you; invest back in them.

- **Undergraduate research exhibitions.** Many undergraduates present their work at a poster session or research showcase. Help them turn their contribution into a poster (see the [poster section](conferences.md#posters) of the conferences guide) — it is great experience and reflects well on the lab.
- **Recommendation letters.** You may be asked to write or contribute to a recommendation letter, or Rebecca may write one informed by your assessment. Pay attention to their growth over the project so you have specifics to offer — vague letters help no one.
- **Career conversations.** Talk with them about what they want next: graduate school, industry, something else. You are one of the closest examples they have of the path just ahead of theirs, and a little honest perspective goes a long way.

Mentoring well is a skill you will use for the rest of your career if you stay in research. The undergraduate in front of you is your first chance to practice it.
