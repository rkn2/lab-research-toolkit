# Measuring Academic Impact

At some point — for an annual review, a fellowship application, a job search, or just to know where you stand — you will need to talk about your scholarly impact in numbers. This guide explains the common metrics, what they actually measure, where to find yours, and how much to care.

**The honest framing first:** metrics are a lossy summary of a body of work, not the work itself. Early in a PhD your numbers will be small, and that is normal — citations lag publication by years. Do not optimize for the metric. Do the good work; the numbers follow. This guide exists so the numbers do not surprise you, not so you chase them.

For a fuller treatment, read Peer Recognized's [Measuring the Academic Success of a Scientist](https://peerrecognized.com/measuring-academic-success-of-a-scientist/) — it covers these metrics and the career-strategy thinking around them in more depth.

## The Metrics

### Citation count
The total number of times your work has been cited. The rawest measure of reach. It grows over your whole career, so it favors people who have been publishing longer — useful for tracking your own trajectory, less useful for comparing people at different career stages.

### h-index
Your **h-index is *h*** if you have *h* papers that have each been cited at least *h* times. Five papers cited 5+ times each = an h-index of 5. It rewards a consistent body of cited work over a single lucky hit, and it does not keep climbing just because one paper goes viral. It is the single number search committees and review panels look at most. Like citation count, it only goes up with time and field size, so compare within a field and career stage, not across them.

### i10-index
The number of your papers with at least 10 citations. Google Scholar reports it. Simple, and a reasonable companion to the h-index for showing breadth.

### Journal metrics (impact factor, quartile, CiteScore)
These describe the *journal*, not your paper. A journal's impact factor is the average citations its articles received over a window. Useful when choosing where to submit (see [Writing a Research Paper](writing-a-paper.md)); a weak proxy for the quality of any individual paper, including yours. Reviewers increasingly discount "I published in a high-IF journal" as a stand-in for impact.

### Altmetrics
Attention outside the citation record: news coverage, policy-document citations, social media, downloads. Captured by the **Altmetric "donut"** and PlumX. For applied and heritage-engineering work that influences practice, codes, or policy, altmetrics can show real-world reach that citation counts miss — worth including when your work has a practitioner audience.

### Field-normalized metrics (FWCI, etc.)
The **Field-Weighted Citation Impact** normalizes your citations against the world average for papers of the same field, type, and year. An FWCI of 1.0 is exactly average; 2.0 is twice the field average. Because it normalizes for field and age, it is fairer for cross-field comparison than a raw h-index. Found in Scopus/SciVal.

## Where to Find Your Numbers

| Source | Free? | Gives you | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Google Scholar** | Yes | Citations, h-index, i10-index | Broadest coverage (indexes conferences, theses, reports). Also inflates a bit by counting non-peer-reviewed sources. The one every student should set up first. |
| **Web of Science** | Via Penn State library | Citations, h-index | Curated, selective; numbers run lower than Scholar but are more defensible. |
| **Scopus** | Via Penn State library | Citations, h-index, FWCI | Good field-normalized metrics via SciVal. |
| **ORCID** | Yes | A persistent author ID + work record | Not a metric — an identity. Disambiguates you from every other person with your name. |
| **Altmetric / PlumX** | Partly | Attention metrics | Often shown on the journal's article page. |

The same person will have three different h-indexes across Scholar, Scopus, and Web of Science because they index different things. That is expected — say which source you are quoting.

## Set Up Your Profiles Early

Do these once, near the start of your PhD, and keep them current. They take an afternoon and pay off for the rest of your career.

- [ ] **ORCID** — register at [orcid.org](https://orcid.org), put the ID on everything (papers, CV, email signature, grant submissions). Penn State and most journals use it.
- [ ] **Google Scholar profile** — create one, set it to auto-update, verify it claims only your papers. This is what people find when they Google you.
- [ ] **Web of Science / Publons (ResearcherID)** — link through the Penn State library so your verified record exists.
- [ ] Keep your **CV** publication list in sync with these (see [Academic Job Search](academic-job-search.md)).

## What to Care About, and When

- **Early PhD:** Your numbers are near zero. Ignore them. Set up the profiles above and focus on getting the first paper out.
- **Mid PhD:** Watch your own trajectory, not absolute numbers. Are citations starting to accrue? Is anyone building on your work?
- **Job market / comprehensive exam:** Know your h-index and citation count on Google Scholar cold — you will be asked, and a vague answer reads as not paying attention. Frame them honestly relative to your career stage.
- **Always:** A single well-cited paper that changed how people think beats a long list of uncited ones. Metrics summarize impact; they do not create it.

If you are unsure how your numbers read for your stage and subfield, ask Rebecca — she can calibrate them against what search committees and panels in heritage and structural engineering actually expect.
