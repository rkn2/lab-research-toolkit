# Academic Job Search

The academic job market is competitive, opaque, and runs on a timeline that most people learn too late. This guide gives you a realistic picture of what the process looks like and how to prepare for it.

## Is an Academic Job Right for You?

An academic position — particularly a tenure-track faculty role — means splitting your time between research, teaching, advising students, writing grants, and service to the department and field. The balance varies by institution type:

- **R1 universities** (like Penn State) weight research and grant funding heavily
- **Teaching-focused universities** weight classroom time and pedagogy
- **Liberal arts colleges** combine both, often with smaller classes and closer student relationships
- **National labs and research institutes** focus almost entirely on research with no teaching obligation

Industry research roles (at companies, government agencies, or consulting firms) offer another path with different tradeoffs: typically more stability and compensation, less autonomy over research direction, and no tenure clock.

Deciding which path is right for you is worth a deliberate conversation with Rebecca before you start preparing materials.

## The Timeline

The academic hiring cycle runs roughly August through March for positions starting the following fall. Most people start preparing materials the summer before they plan to go on the market, which typically means the summer of your final or penultimate year.

| Month | Activity |
|---|---|
| May–August | Draft and revise application materials; ask for reference letters |
| September | Job ads appear; begin submitting applications |
| October–November | Peak application period; most deadlines fall here |
| November–December | Phone and video interviews |
| January–February | Campus visits |
| February–March | Offers and negotiations |

Starting this timeline without any publications is very difficult. The job search works best when you have at least one or two published or accepted journal papers.

## The Application Materials

### Research Statement

The research statement explains what you have done, what you plan to do next, and why it matters. It should be written for a faculty search committee — meaning some members will be in your area, and some will not.

Typical structure:
1. An opening that states your research agenda clearly (not a list of topics — a coherent story)
2. Summary of your dissertation work and its contributions
3. Near-term research directions and specific projects you would pursue
4. Longer-term vision and why this matters to the field
5. Fit with the department, if the ad suggests a specific focus area

Most research statements are 2–4 pages. Do not go longer unless the ad specifies otherwise.

### Teaching Statement

The teaching statement describes your approach to teaching and your experience in the classroom. Even at R1 institutions, this matters.

Cover: your teaching philosophy (brief), specific courses you are prepared to teach at both the undergraduate and graduate levels, how you approach mentoring students, and any evidence of teaching effectiveness (evaluations, course design, mentoring outcomes).

Most teaching statements are 1–2 pages.

### Cover Letter

The cover letter is one page. It introduces you, names the specific position you are applying for, summarizes your research agenda and teaching qualifications, and explains why you are a good fit for this particular department. Tailor it for each application — a generic letter reads as one.

### CV

Your academic CV is not a resume. It is a complete record of your scholarly work: education, publications, presentations, funding, teaching experience, service, and honors. There is no page limit. Keep it up to date throughout your program.

Publications are the most important section. List them in reverse chronological order, with your name in the author list exactly as it appears. Distinguish between peer-reviewed journal papers, conference papers, and papers under review.

### Writing Sample / Representative Publication

Some applications ask for a representative publication. Send your best journal paper — the one that best represents your research contributions and writing quality.

### References

You will need three to five reference letters. Choose people who know your research well and can speak specifically to your contributions — not just your general qualities. Your advisor writes one. The others typically come from committee members or close collaborators.

Ask your references early — at least two to three months before your first deadline. Send them your materials and the specific positions you are applying to so they can tailor their letters.

## Finding Positions

Job ads for engineering and architectural engineering faculty positions are posted on:

- **HigherEdJobs.com** — broadest coverage
- **The Chronicle of Higher Education** (chronicle.com/jobs)
- **Disciplinary society job boards** — ASCE, EERI, SAH, and others depending on your subfield
- **Direct department websites** — some postings do not appear on aggregators; check departments you are interested in directly

Track your applications in a spreadsheet: institution, position, deadline, status, and materials sent. Applications pile up fast.

## The Interview Process

### The Phone or Video Screen

The screen is typically 30–45 minutes with a subcommittee. They will ask about your research, your teaching, and why you are interested in their department. Prepare a clear, two-minute version of your research story. Have questions ready — they always ask.

### The Campus Visit

If you are invited for a campus visit, the department is seriously interested. A typical visit is one to two days and includes:

- **Job talk:** A 45–60 minute public presentation of your research, usually open to the whole department. This is the most important part of the visit. Prepare it like a conference keynote — polished, clear, and accessible to a broad engineering audience.
- **Chalk talk or research vision presentation:** Some departments ask separately for a 20–30 minute informal discussion of your future research plans and grant strategy.
- **One-on-one meetings** with individual faculty — typically 6–10 back-to-back conversations. These are conversations, not interviews. Ask about their work. Find genuine connections.
- **Meeting with the chair** — often includes a conversation about startup, course load, and expectations.
- **Lunch and dinner** — still part of the evaluation. Be engaged and collegial throughout.

The department is evaluating fit as much as qualifications. They are asking: would I want this person as a colleague for the next 30 years?

## Evaluating and Negotiating Offers

An offer from a university is a starting point, not a final number. Most things are negotiable:

- **Startup funds** — for equipment, personnel, and travel in your first years before grants come in
- **Course release** — reduced teaching load in year one or two
- **Moving expenses**
- **Salary** — especially at private institutions; public university salaries may be constrained by state scales
- **Lab space**
- **Postdoc or graduate student support** before your first grants

Get everything in writing before you accept. If you have multiple offers, it is acceptable to use them as leverage, but be straightforward with each department about your timeline and situation.

## If You Do Not Get an Offer

A difficult market year is not a verdict on your quality as a researcher. Options:

- **Postdoc:** A postdoc lets you publish more, expand your network, and reapply with a stronger record. Common in many fields.
- **Industry research:** Many alumni return to academia after 2–5 years in industry with a broader perspective and sometimes better funding connections.
- **Reapplying:** The job market shifts year to year. A year out of the cycle, with new publications, often changes the outcome.

Talk with Rebecca honestly about the landscape before you go on the market, and again if you do not get where you hoped to go.
