# How to Read a Paper

Reading academic papers is a skill. Most people read them wrong — starting at the beginning and plowing through to the end, spending equal time on every section. This is slow and often unproductive. Here is a better approach.

---

## The Three-Pass Method

### Pass 1: The 5-Minute Skim (decide if it's worth your time)

Read only:
1. The title and abstract
2. The introduction (first and last paragraph)
3. The section headings
4. The figures and their captions
5. The conclusion

After 5 minutes you should be able to answer: What problem does this paper address? What did they find? Is this relevant to what I'm working on?

If yes, continue to Pass 2. If not, move on.

### Pass 2: The Careful Read (understand what they did)

Read the whole paper but skip the math and implementation details for now. Focus on:
- What is the research question?
- What data did they use and where did it come from?
- What method did they use and why?
- What are the main results?
- What do the authors say are the limitations?

Take notes. Write down questions. Mark anything you don't understand — you'll come back to it.

Goal: You should be able to explain this paper to a labmate after Pass 2.

### Pass 3: The Deep Read (if you really need to understand it)

Go through the methods and math in detail. Try to reproduce key results mentally. Read the related work section carefully to understand how this paper positions itself in the field.

Reserve Pass 3 for papers that are directly foundational to your research.

---

## What to Look For

**In the introduction:**
- What gap does this paper claim to fill?
- How do they describe related work — are there papers cited that you should also read?

**In the methods:**
- Could you reproduce this? If not, what is missing?
- Are there assumptions baked in that might not hold in your context?

**In the results:**
- Do the figures actually support the claims?
- Are the baselines fair? Is the comparison meaningful?
- Are error bars and uncertainty reported?

**In the discussion and conclusion:**
- What do the authors say their work cannot do?
- What future work do they suggest? (This is often a map of open problems.)

---

## Critical Reading

Not everything published is correct, well-designed, or applicable to your work. Ask:

- Is the dataset large enough and representative enough to support the conclusions?
- Are there confounds the authors did not account for?
- Does the method generalize, or does it only work in the specific conditions tested?
- Is this published in a credible venue? (See the journal quality notes in `finding-literature.md`)

Healthy skepticism is not cynicism — it is the job.

---

## Taking Notes

Do not just highlight. Write something down in your own words. A sentence or two per paper is enough: what the paper does, what you found interesting or surprising, and how it connects to your work.

A Zotero note attached to the paper is fine. What matters is that the note is in your own words, not copied text. If you cannot paraphrase the main contribution, you do not yet understand it.

---

## Building on What You Read

When you find a useful paper, do not stop there:

- Check its references for older foundational work you should know
- Check who has cited it (Google Scholar "Cited by") for newer work building on it
- Add it to Research Rabbit to find related papers in the same cluster
- Ask yourself: how does this change what I'm doing, if at all?

The goal is not to read as many papers as possible. It is to develop a clear picture of what is known, what is debated, and where the open questions are in your specific area.
