# AI Tools for Research

AI assistants are now a standard part of academic research workflows. This guide covers the main tools available, what they are good for, and how to get started. The goal is not to tell you which one to use — they are all capable — but to make sure you understand your options and can use any of them effectively.

---

## The Main Tools

### ChatGPT (OpenAI)
- **Access:** chatgpt.com — free tier available, paid tiers unlock more capable models
- **Strengths:** widely used, large community of examples online, good for general writing and brainstorming
- **Good for:** drafting text, summarizing documents, explaining concepts, working through ideas

### Claude (Anthropic)
- **Access:** claude.ai — free tier available, paid tiers unlock longer context and more capability
- **Strengths:** handles very long documents well, strong at careful reasoning and following nuanced instructions
- **Good for:** reading and synthesizing long papers, structured writing tasks, detailed feedback on drafts
- **Claude Code:** a command-line version of Claude that can read and edit files on your computer directly — useful if you are working with code or large sets of documents

### Gemini (Google)
- **Access:** gemini.google.com — free tier available
- **Strengths:** integrated with Google Workspace (Docs, Drive, Gmail), good at multimodal tasks involving images
- **Good for:** tasks where your materials already live in Google Drive, analyzing figures and images

## AI Inside Your Other Tools

Many tools you may already use have AI built in:

- **GitHub Copilot** — AI assistance inside VS Code and other code editors; suggests code as you type and can explain or rewrite code on request
- **Notion AI** — if you use Notion for notes, AI is built into the editor
- **Microsoft Copilot** — built into Word, Excel, and Teams if your institution uses Microsoft 365

## How to Use Any of These Well

The quality of what you get out depends almost entirely on how clearly you describe what you need. A few principles that apply to every tool:

**Be specific about your goal.** "Help me with my paper" will produce generic output. "I am writing the introduction to a paper about tornado damage to unreinforced masonry buildings. Here is my current draft. What is missing and what is unclear?" will produce something useful.

**Give it context.** Paste in the relevant text, paper, or data. Do not assume it knows your project.

**Explain the why, not just the what.** "Rewrite this paragraph" is less useful than "Rewrite this paragraph — it needs to be clearer about why dimensionality reduction matters for this dataset, for an audience of civil engineers who are not ML experts."

**Treat the output as a draft, not a final answer.** AI tools make things up, misread sources, and confidently produce errors. Always verify claims against the original source.

**Iterate.** If the first response is not quite right, say so. "That is close but too technical — simplify it" or "You missed the main point, which is X" will get you much further than starting over.

## A Note on Attribution and Integrity

Follow Penn State's academic integrity guidelines and the policies of any journal or conference you are submitting to. When in doubt, disclose. Using AI to help you think and write is increasingly accepted; passing AI-generated text off as your own original analysis without disclosure is not.
