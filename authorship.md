# Authorship and Collaboration

Authorship disputes are one of the most common and most bitter sources of conflict in academic research, and almost all of them are avoidable. They happen because people leave authorship unspoken until the paper is nearly done, and by then everyone has a different story in their head about who deserves what. The fix is simple: **talk about authorship early, explicitly, and in writing.**

This guide covers who counts as an author, how author order works, and the norms in this lab. When in doubt about any specific case, ask Rebecca — she makes the final call on lab papers.

## Who Counts as an Author

Authorship means intellectual contribution to the work, not mere presence near it. The widely used standard (from the ICMJE, adapted across fields) is that an author has meaningfully contributed to the research *and* to the writing, has approved the final version, and takes responsibility for the work.

In practice, in engineering research:

- **Authors** are people who contributed intellectually — designing the study, developing methods, running analysis, interpreting results, writing the paper.
- **Not automatically authors:** someone who gave you a dataset, ran a machine for you, funded the work, or holds a title above you. Providing a resource or a paycheck is not, by itself, authorship — though it is often an acknowledgment, and sometimes these people *do* contribute intellectually and earn authorship on those grounds.

The line is intellectual contribution to *this paper*, not seniority, funding, or friendship.

## The CRediT Taxonomy

Many journals now use **CRediT** (Contributor Roles Taxonomy) to make contributions explicit. Instead of arguing about who "did more," each author's specific roles are listed. The 14 roles include:

- Conceptualization
- Methodology
- Software
- Validation
- Formal analysis
- Investigation
- Data curation
- Writing – original draft
- Writing – review & editing
- Visualization
- Supervision
- Funding acquisition

CRediT is useful even when a journal does not require it, because it turns a vague status question ("am I an author?") into a concrete one ("which of these did I do?"). Early in a project, sketching out who owns which roles is one of the cleanest ways to set authorship expectations without it feeling personal.

## Author Order

Author order carries meaning, and the conventions vary by field. In engineering and the physical sciences, the common pattern is:

- **First author:** the person who did the bulk of the work and wrote the paper. For your own research, this is you. First authorship is the currency of a PhD — it is what committees, hiring panels, and fellowship reviewers look at.
- **Middle authors:** contributors, roughly in order of contribution.
- **Last author:** typically the senior researcher or PI who supervised the work — often your adviser. In many fields the last-author position is a position of seniority, not an afterthought.

## Committee Members and Collaborators

- **Committee members** are not automatically co-authors on your papers. They advise your overall research; that is a different role from contributing to a specific paper. A committee member becomes a co-author only if they contribute intellectually to that particular piece of work — and if they do, they earn it like anyone else.
- **External collaborators** who contribute methods, analysis, or writing are authors. Settle their inclusion and order *before* the writing is underway, ideally when the collaboration begins.
- **Undergraduate mentees** who make a real intellectual contribution can and should be authors. If an undergraduate you mentored did substantive work, include them — early-career authorship matters enormously to them. See [Mentoring an Undergraduate Researcher](mentoring-undergrads.md).

## Acknowledgments vs. Authorship

Not everyone who helped is an author, but everyone who helped should be recognized. The acknowledgments section is where you thank people whose contribution did not rise to authorship:

- People who gave feedback, advice, or a useful conversation
- Technical staff who ran equipment or provided samples
- People who shared data you used
- **Funding sources** — grants must be acknowledged, usually with the grant number. Check with Rebecca for the correct wording for the grant that supported your work.

Being thorough and generous in acknowledgments costs nothing and prevents hurt feelings. When unsure whether someone is an author or an acknowledgment, the CRediT question settles it: did they contribute an intellectual role to this paper, or did they help around the edges?

## Have the Conversation Early

Every problem this guide addresses is solved by one habit: **discuss authorship at the start of a collaboration, not the end.** When you begin a project or bring someone in:

1. Talk about who is likely to be an author and in what order.
2. Write it down — even a short email or a shared note listing expected authors and CRediT roles.
3. Revisit it if contributions change. If someone ends up doing far more or less than expected, adjust the plan openly rather than letting resentment build.

None of this is awkward if you do it early and treat it as normal project planning. It only becomes awkward when it is avoided until the stakes are high. When a case is genuinely unclear, bring it to Rebecca before it becomes a conflict — that is exactly the kind of thing advisers are for.
