# Finding and Organizing Literature

Finding good papers is a skill. This guide walks you through how to discover literature, how to expand from one good paper to many related ones, how to stay organized, and what to watch out for.

---

## Finding Papers

### Google Scholar
**scholar.google.com**

The starting point for most literature searches. Type in keywords, author names, or paste in a title. A few things worth knowing:

- **"Cited by"** — click this under any paper to see every paper that has cited it. This is one of the best ways to find newer work building on a foundational paper.
- **Set up alerts** — search for a topic and click "Create alert" to get email notifications when new papers matching that search are published.
- **Author profiles** — click an author's name to see their full publication list and find other work from the same group.

### Research Rabbit
**researchrabbitapp.com** — free

This is one of the most useful tools for expanding from a paper you already trust. You add a paper (or a collection of papers) and Research Rabbit shows you:

- Papers that cite your paper
- Papers your paper cites
- Papers that are "similar" based on citation overlap
- Co-author networks

**Workflow:** Start with 2-3 papers you know are relevant and high quality. Add them to Research Rabbit. Use the visualization to identify clusters of related work and find the key papers in your area you may have missed.

## Organizing Your Papers

### Zotero
**zotero.org** — free

The recommended tool for managing your reference library. Zotero lets you:

- Save papers with one click from your browser (install the browser extension)
- Organize papers into folders and tag them
- Generate citations and bibliographies in any format
- Sync your library across devices
- Annotate PDFs inside the app

**Get started:** Install Zotero and the browser extension. Every time you find a paper worth keeping, click the extension button to save it. Build the habit early — retroactively organizing a library of 200 papers is painful.

### Mendeley
**mendeley.com** — free (owned by Elsevier)

Similar to Zotero. Some people prefer its interface. Worth knowing it exists, especially if collaborators are already using it. Zotero is generally recommended for new users because it is fully open source and not tied to a publisher.

---

## Suggested Workflow

1. Identify 2-3 high-quality, highly-cited papers on your topic using Google Scholar
2. Save them to Zotero
3. Add them to Research Rabbit and explore the citation network
4. Save anything relevant to Zotero as you go
5. Use Google Scholar alerts to catch new work going forward

## A Word on Journal Quality

Not all journals are equal. Some journals will accept almost anything for a fee — these are called predatory journals. Publishing in them, or citing heavily from them, damages your credibility.

**Be cautious with MDPI journals** (e.g., *Buildings*, *Sensors*, *Materials*). MDPI is a large open-access publisher with a fast review process and high acceptance rates. Some MDPI journals are legitimate and well-regarded; others are not. Before citing a paper from an MDPI journal, check whether it is indexed in reputable databases (Web of Science, Scopus) and look critically at the quality of the work itself.

**General rule:** if a journal sent you an unsolicited email inviting you to submit, be skeptical. Check the journal's impact factor and whether it appears on recognized lists like the Web of Science or Scopus.

When in doubt, ask Rebecca before relying heavily on a source.
