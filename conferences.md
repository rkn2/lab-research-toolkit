# Conferences and Posters

Conferences are where you meet your field. They are how you learn what people are working on before it is published, get feedback on work in progress, practice presenting in a low-stakes setting, and — not incidentally — meet the people who will one day review your papers, write your recommendation letters, and sit on your hiring committees. Going to conferences is part of your job as a researcher, not a reward for finishing one.

This guide covers the mechanics that [Giving a Presentation](giving-a-presentation.md) does not: choosing conferences, writing abstracts, making posters, and the social and logistical side of attending.

## Why and When to Go

Different stages of your PhD call for different conference roles:

- **Early (Years 1–2):** Go to *listen*. Present a poster if you have preliminary work. The goal is to learn the landscape, see how people talk about the field, and start recognizing names and faces.
- **Middle (Years 2–4):** Present talks on your papers. This is where conference presentations and journal papers reinforce each other — a conference paper is often a first public airing of work you will later develop into a journal submission.
- **Late (Year 4–5):** Present your strongest work and, critically, *be seen*. If you are going on the academic job market, the conferences the year before you apply are where you become a known quantity to the people who will hire you. See [Academic Job Search](academic-job-search.md).

**How to evaluate a conference you are considering:**
- Who from your field actually attends? Look at last year's program.
- Is it archival (produces citable proceedings) or presentation-only? Both have value, but know which one you are getting.
- Does it have a student paper or poster competition? These are worth targeting — they come with visibility and sometimes travel money.
- What is the deadline, and does your work fit the timeline? Abstract deadlines are often six-plus months before the event.

## Writing a Conference Abstract

Most conferences select presentations from a short abstract submitted months ahead, often before your results are final. A conference abstract is a promise about what you will present. Write it to be accepted and to be honest about what you will actually have done.

A conference abstract, in ~200–300 words, does the same job as a paper abstract in miniature:

1. **The problem** — one or two sentences of motivation.
2. **The gap** — what is missing in current approaches.
3. **Your approach** — what you did or are doing.
4. **Results** — what you found, or what you expect to show. If results are preliminary, it is fine to say "preliminary results indicate…" but do not promise findings you are not confident you will have by the conference.
5. **Significance** — why the audience should care.

Reuse the discipline from [Writing a Research Paper](writing-a-paper.md): one clear message, stated plainly. Avoid promising more than you can deliver — an over-sold abstract leads to an under-delivering talk, and this is a small world.

## Posters

Posters are their own medium, not a paper taped to a board. A good poster works from across the room and supports a conversation up close.

**Layout:**
- **One clear message.** A viewer walking past should grasp your main point in the time it takes to walk past — roughly 30 seconds. Everything serves that message.
- **Figures over text.** A poster is mostly visual. Big, clean figures (see [Making Figures](making-figures.md)); minimal blocks of prose. If a paragraph can be a figure, make it a figure.
- **Readable from a distance.** Title readable from across the room; body text readable from a few feet away. If you are unsure, it is too small.
- **A clear visual path.** Guide the eye: title → motivation → approach → results → takeaway. Number sections or use arrows if the flow is not obvious.
- **Your name, the lab, and contact info.** Include the lab template branding and a way to follow up. A QR code to the paper or your page works well.

Use the lab poster/slide template for consistent branding: [templates/labTemplate.pptx](templates/labTemplate.pptx).

**At the poster session:** Have a 30-second version and a 3-minute version of your pitch ready. Most people want the 30-second one. Do not read your poster to people — talk *with* them, and let their questions steer. A poster session is the most interactive presentation format you will ever get; the conversations are the point.

## Networking Without Dread

Most researchers find networking uncomfortable. It is a learnable skill, not a personality trait, and the academic version is gentler than the corporate one — everyone is there because they are interested in the same narrow thing you are.

- **Have an opener.** "What are you working on?" is the entire secret. People love talking about their research. You mostly need to ask and listen.
- **Go to the talks in your area and talk to the speaker afterward.** "I really liked your point about X — I'm working on something related" is a complete, welcome introduction.
- **Use your adviser.** Rebecca knows people. Ask her to introduce you to researchers whose work matters to yours. A warm introduction removes all the awkwardness.
- **Follow up.** The value of a conference contact is realized *after* the conference — see below.
- **Take care of yourself.** Conferences are exhausting for introverts. It is fine to skip a session to recharge. Three good conversations beat twenty forced ones.

## After the Conference

The conference is not over when you fly home. The follow-up is where most of the long-term value lives:

- **Send follow-up emails within a week.** To anyone you had a real conversation with: a short note referencing what you discussed, and anything you promised to share. This is how a hallway chat becomes a collaborator or a reference.
- **Write up what you learned.** Which talks changed how you think? Which papers do you now need to read? Bring this to your next [weekly meeting](weekly-meetings.md).
- **Turn the conference paper into a journal paper.** If you presented work at an archival conference, that is often the seed of a journal submission. Develop it — do not let it sit as a conference paper forever. See [Writing a Research Paper](writing-a-paper.md).
