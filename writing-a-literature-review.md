# Writing a Literature Review

Finding and reading papers is one skill. Turning what you have read into a literature review is a different one, and it is where most students stumble. A literature review is not a list of papers with a paragraph about each. It is an argument, built from other people's work, that ends by pointing at the gap your research fills.

This guide sits between [Finding and Organizing Literature](finding-literature.md) (how to find and store papers) and the [comprehensive exam proposal](phd-milestones.md#comprehensive-exam) (which requires a literature review that ends in an explicit gap statement). ## Synthesis, Not Summary

The trap that catches almost everyone is the **annotated bibliography disguised as a review**: paragraph after paragraph of "Smith (2019) did X. Jones (2020) did Y. Lee (2021) did Z." Each paragraph is about one paper. The reader learns what everyone did but not what it *means*, how the works relate, or where the field actually stands.

A real literature review does the opposite. Each paragraph is about an *idea*, and papers are cited in support of it:

> Early damage-assessment methods relied on manual field surveys, which are accurate but slow and dangerous in post-disaster conditions (Smith 2019; Jones 2020). More recent work has turned to remote sensing to address the speed problem (Lee 2021; Patel 2022), though these approaches struggle to detect interior structural damage.

Notice: the paragraph is organized around a claim about the field, and three papers are marshaled to support it. The reader learns the *shape of the conversation*, not just its contents. That is synthesis.

The test: **if you could reorder your paragraphs without anything breaking, you are summarizing, not synthesizing.** A synthesized review has an argument that builds, so the order matters.

## Organize by Theme, Not by Paper

Before you write a word, decide on the themes your review is organized around. These are the sub-questions or sub-topics that structure the conversation in your field. For a hazards-and-heritage project they might be:

- Approaches to measuring the exposure (the hazard side)
- Approaches to measuring vulnerability (the structure side)
- Methods for combining the two into risk
- The specific gap around historic or unreinforced-masonry structures

Each becomes a subsection. Within each subsection, you synthesize the relevant papers into an argument. This is exactly the structure the comprehensive proposal asks for: *"Literature review — organized into subsections by topic. End with a summary and explicit statement of the research gap."*

## The Synthesis Matrix

The synthesis matrix is the single most useful tool for getting from a pile of papers to a themed argument. Build a table:

- **Rows:** the papers you have read
- **Columns:** the themes, concepts, or dimensions you care about

| Paper | Hazard model | Vulnerability model | Structure type | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Smith 2019 | Deterministic | Empirical fragility | Modern RC | No historic structures |
| Jones 2020 | Probabilistic | Expert judgment | Mixed | Small sample |
| Lee 2021 | Probabilistic | ML-based | URM | Single hazard only |

Fill this in as you read (it pairs naturally with your Zotero notes from [Finding and Organizing Literature](finding-literature.md)). Two things happen:

1. **Reading down a column reveals your paragraphs.** Everything in the "Vulnerability model" column is the raw material for your "approaches to vulnerability" subsection — and the variety in that column is the synthesis.
2. **Empty or sparse cells reveal your gap.** If the "historic structures" cells are mostly empty, or every paper handles a single hazard, you have just found — and can *prove* — the gap your research fills.

The matrix turns "I have a vague sense no one has done this" into "here is a table demonstrating that no one has done this." That is far more convincing to a committee and to reviewers.

## Building Toward the Gap

A literature review is a funnel, like the introduction of a paper. It moves from the broad state of the field to the specific, defensible opening your work occupies:

1. **Establish the landscape.** What are the major approaches, and what do they get right? Give the field its due — a review that only criticizes reads as arrogant and uninformed.
2. **Expose the tensions and limits.** Where do approaches disagree? What do they consistently fail to handle? This is where the synthesis matrix pays off.
3. **State the gap explicitly.** End with a clear, direct statement: *"While prior work has addressed X and Y, no existing approach handles Z. This research addresses that gap."* Do not make the reader infer it. Name it.

That final gap statement is not a throwaway sentence — it is the hinge the entire proposal and paper turn on. Your research questions answer it, your methods address it, your contributions fill it. Write it so it is impossible to miss.

## How Much Is Enough

New researchers worry endlessly about whether they have read enough. Useful signals that your review is adequately complete:

- **You keep seeing the same papers cited.** When new papers you find mostly cite work you already know, you have covered the core of the field. This is the clearest signal.
- **You can name the major camps and their tradeoffs** without looking anything up.
- **You can state the gap in one sentence** and defend it against "hasn't so-and-so done that?"

You will never read everything, and you do not need to. You need enough to establish the landscape and prove the gap. Beyond that, more reading has sharply diminishing returns — and at some point, reading more becomes a way of avoiding writing. If you can pass the three tests above, start writing.

## Staying Current

A literature review is written once but must stay alive. New relevant papers will appear between your comps and your defense.

- **Set up alerts.** Google Scholar alerts for your key search terms and for citations of the foundational papers in your area push new work to you automatically. See [Finding and Organizing Literature](finding-literature.md).
- **Keep the matrix going.** When a relevant paper appears, add a row. Ask whether it fills your gap (a problem to address in your framing) or reinforces it (more support for your motivation).
- **Revisit before each milestone.** Update the review before your comps, before submitting each paper, and before your defense, so a committee member cannot surface a 2024 paper you have never heard of.
