## The Core Idea: Start With the Results

Most people write a paper in the order it will be read: introduction, methods, results, discussion, conclusion. This is a mistake. You should write it in roughly the opposite order, starting from what you actually know and working outward to context.

The reason is simple: your results are the most concrete thing you have. You understand them. Everything else in the paper — the framing, the argument, the context — exists to explain why those results matter. If you start with the introduction, you are trying to build a frame before you know what you are framing.

## The LEAP Writing Order

The guide introduces a framework called LEAP. Write in this order:

### 1. Lay Out the Facts — *Start here*
Make your figures, tables, and charts first. Get your results into visual form and write the Results section. Describe what you see — declarative, factual, no interpretation yet. This is the foundation everything else is built on.

### 2. Explain the Results
Write the Discussion, then the Conclusion, then the Methods. Now that your results are clear, you can explain what they mean, what they imply, and how you got there. Distill your paper's central message into one or two sentences before writing the Discussion — if you cannot state the main point in two sentences, you are not ready to write it yet.

### 3. Advertise the Research
Write the Introduction last. The Introduction is a funnel: start broad (the big problem), narrow to the specific gap in knowledge, then arrive at your specific approach. Writing it last means you know exactly what gap your paper fills, because you have already written the paper that fills it.

Then write the Abstract, Title, and any highlights or graphical abstract.

### 4. Prepare for Submission
Choose the right journal before you submit, not after. Read the author guidelines. Edit with fresh eyes — take at least a day away from the paper before your final read. Write a cover letter that explains why this paper belongs in this journal specifically.

## A Few Things Rebecca Adds

**Your figures drive the paper.** If you follow the LEAP approach, you will start every paper by making figures. This is intentional. A paper with clear, well-made figures almost writes itself — the text is explaining what the reader can already see. A paper with weak or unclear figures is a warning sign that the results are not yet understood well enough to write about.

**One paper, one message.** Every paper should have a single central claim that you can state in two sentences. If yours has three, you have three papers. Resist the urge to include everything — the goal is clarity, not comprehensiveness.

**Write badly first.** The first draft does not need to be good. It needs to exist. Write fast and ugly to get the structure down, then revise. Writers who try to write perfectly on the first pass usually do not write at all.

**Reviewer feedback is not personal.** It is information. When you get reviews back, read them once, step away, then read them again. Respond to every point — even if you disagree, explain why clearly and respectfully. The goal is publication, not winning an argument.

## The Structure of a Paper (for reference)

Even though you will not write in this order, your paper will be read in this order:

| Section | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Title | Make the contribution and topic clear |
| Abstract | Self-contained summary: problem, approach, result, significance |
| Introduction | Context → gap in knowledge → your approach |
| Methods | How you did it — enough detail to replicate |
| Results | What you found — figures first, text describes them |
| Discussion | What it means, limitations, how it fits the literature |
| Conclusion | The main message, concisely |
| References | Everything cited, nothing extra |
