# Responding to Peer Review

You submitted a paper. Weeks or months later, the decision arrives with reviewer comments attached. This is one of the most stressful moments in a young researcher's life, and almost no one is taught how to handle it well. This guide is how.

## What the Decision Means

Journal decisions come in a small number of flavors. Learn to read them correctly, because the emotional weight of the words does not match their actual meaning.

| Decision | What it actually means |
|---|---|
| **Accept** | Rare on first submission. Celebrate, then check for any final copy-edits. |
| **Minor revision** | The paper will almost certainly be published. Address the comments cleanly and it is done. |
| **Major revision** | **This is good news.** The editor sees a publishable paper that needs work. Most published papers went through at least one major revision. Treat it as an invitation, not a rejection. |
| **Reject and resubmit** | The editor is interested but the current version has fundamental problems. A new submission, but with a foot in the door. |
| **Reject** | Not accepted here. Sometimes about fit rather than quality. Read the comments, improve the paper, submit elsewhere. A reject is a redirection, not a verdict on your worth. |

The single most important reframe: **major revision is the normal path to publication, not a setback.** New researchers often read "major revision" as "they hated it." They did not. They spent hours reading your work and telling you how to make it publishable.

## The First Read

When the decision lands, follow a protocol instead of your instincts:

1. **Read the comments once, then step away.** Do not respond, do not start editing, do not email your co-authors in a panic. Reviewer comments almost always feel harsher on the first read than they are. Give it a day.
2. **Read again with a highlighter.** On the second pass, every comment is one of three things: (a) a change you agree with, (b) a change you disagree with, (c) a misunderstanding of what you wrote. All three are actionable. Even a misunderstanding is useful information — if a careful expert reader misread you, so will everyone else, so the text needs to change regardless of who was "right."
3. **Make a list.** Number every distinct point across all reviewers. Long comments often contain three or four separate requests hiding in one paragraph — split them out. This numbered list becomes the backbone of your response document.

By the end of the first read you should feel calmer, because a wall of criticism has become a finite, numbered to-do list.

## The Response Document

You will write a point-by-point response letter that goes back to the editor alongside your revised manuscript. This document is often what determines whether the paper is accepted on the next round, so treat it as seriously as the paper itself.

Use the template: **[templates/response-to-reviewers.md](templates/response-to-reviewers.md)**

The format for every single comment is the same three parts:

1. **Quote the reviewer's comment verbatim.** The editor should never have to flip back to the original review to know what you are responding to.
2. **Respond.** Say what you did, or explain your reasoning if you did not make the change.
3. **Show the change.** Quote the new or revised text, and point to where it now lives (e.g., "see Section 3.2, page 7"). Make it effortless for the reviewer to verify you did what you claim.

Principles that make a response letter work:

- **Answer every point.** Every one. Skipping a comment reads as either sloppiness or evasion, and reviewers notice immediately.
- **Be gracious, even when you disagree.** Open with genuine thanks. The reviewers volunteered their time. A warm, professional tone costs nothing and buys goodwill.
- **Make the changes visible.** Reviewers are busy and slightly annoyed to be back here. The easier you make it to see that you addressed everything, the faster and more favorably they respond.
- **Quote your own new text.** Do not just say "we clarified this." Paste the clarified sentence. Show, don't assert.

## How to Disagree With a Reviewer

You will sometimes get a comment that is wrong, out of scope, or would make the paper worse. You are allowed to push back. Do it well:

- **Concede the valid kernel first.** Most bad comments contain a real observation underneath a bad prescription. Acknowledge what the reviewer noticed before explaining why you are handling it differently.
- **Argue with evidence, not opinion.** "We respectfully disagree" is not an argument. "We disagree because [citation / result / methodological reason]" is. Bring data.
- **Offer a middle path.** If a reviewer wants an analysis you think is out of scope, consider adding a sentence acknowledging it as future work rather than fighting the request outright. A small concession often closes the point.
- **Stay respectful no matter what.** Even if a comment is careless or the reviewer clearly skimmed, your response is read by the editor, who decides your fate. Never let frustration into the text. The goal is publication, not winning the argument.

If a requested change is genuinely impossible or wrong, it is the editor — not the reviewer — you are ultimately persuading. Write your justification for the editor's eyes.

## When Reviewers Contradict Each Other

It happens constantly: Reviewer 1 wants the methods section expanded, Reviewer 2 wants it cut. When reviewers conflict:

- **Name the conflict explicitly** in your response to both, so the editor sees you noticed rather than ignored one.
- **Make a judgment call and justify it.** Pick the change that best serves the paper and explain your reasoning to both reviewers.
- **Ask the editor if you are truly stuck.** The editor arbitrates. A short note — "Reviewers 1 and 2 requested opposite changes to Section 4; we have taken approach X for reason Y, but are happy to reconsider" — is completely legitimate.

## Revising the Manuscript

- **Use tracked changes or a diff.** Many journals want a marked-up version showing what changed. For LaTeX, `latexdiff` generates a version with additions and deletions marked automatically — learn it once, use it forever. See [LaTeX and Overleaf](latex-and-overleaf.md).
- **Keep your response letter and your edits in sync.** Every "we changed X" in the letter must correspond to a real, findable change in the manuscript. Do a final pass reading the letter with the paper open, verifying each claim.
- **Do not introduce new problems.** In your rush to satisfy Reviewer 2, do not break something Reviewer 3 was happy with. Re-read the whole paper after revising.

## The Resubmission Cover Letter

A short note to the editor accompanies the resubmission. It is brief:

- Thank the editor and reviewers.
- State that you have addressed all comments and that a point-by-point response is attached.
- Note anything the editor specifically should know (e.g., a co-author change, or a comment you were unable to fully address and why).

Keep it to a few sentences. The real work is in the response document.
